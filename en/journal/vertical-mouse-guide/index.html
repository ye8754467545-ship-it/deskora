Vertical mice: who they suit and how to choose one

A standard mouse keeps your palm facing down. To do that, the two forearm bones rotate and cross. A vertical mouse holds your hand in a handshake-like position in which the forearm rotates less.

Who is it for?

If your forearm or wrist feels tired after long days of mousing, trying a vertical mouse makes sense. It is not a medical treatment, though. If you have pain, numbness or tingling, see a doctor first.

Hand size

A vertical mouse should suit your hand. One that is too small makes your fingers curl; one that is too large keeps your hand stretched. Many makers state a hand-size range for each model; measure from your wrist crease to the tip of your middle finger and compare.

Left or right hand

Unlike many standard mice, a vertical mouse is not symmetrical. If you are left-handed, look for a left-handed version, which not every model offers.

Angle and precision

Vertical mice come at different angles. A gentler angle is closer to a standard mouse and easier to get used to. For precise work such as design, expect a few days of practice. Move from your forearm rather than only your wrist.

Placement matters too

Even the best mouse pulls your shoulder forward if it sits far from the keyboard. Keep it beside the keyboard at the same height (see our keyboard and mouse position guide).

  • Check the stated hand-size range.
  • Find a left-handed version if you need one.
  • Give yourself a few days to a week to adjust.
  • Read the return terms.

This is general guidance and does not replace advice from a doctor or specialist. If you have persistent pain, speak to a professional.
