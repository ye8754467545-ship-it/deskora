How to sit properly at your desk

“Sit up straight” is advice everyone has heard, but few people know what straight actually means. OSHA’s guide to computer workstations uses the term “neutral posture”: a position in which your joints are naturally aligned and muscles and tendons carry the least strain.

Start at the bottom: feet

Your feet should rest fully on the floor. If they do not reach once the chair height is right, a footrest solves it. Your thighs should be roughly parallel to the floor and your knees about level with your hips, with your feet slightly forward of your knees.

Hips and back

Sit all the way back so the backrest can support your lower back. Your torso and neck should be roughly vertical and in line. You do not need to sit rigidly upright: leaning back a little is perfectly fine if the backrest supports you well.

Shoulders, elbows and wrists

Let your shoulders relax and keep your elbows close to your body. OSHA suggests an elbow angle between 90 and 120 degrees. Hands, wrists and forearms should be straight, in line and roughly parallel to the floor. If you lift your shoulders to reach the keyboard, the desk or keyboard is too high for you.

Head and eyes

Your head should balance on your neck, not jut forward. If you lean towards the screen, it is usually too far away, too low, or the text is too small. We cover screen distance and height in a separate monitor guide.

The best posture is the next one

Even the best posture becomes tiring if held for hours. Cornell University’s ergonomics guidance stresses changing position every 20 to 30 minutes and moving for a couple of minutes. Simply standing is not enough; movement is what matters.

A quick check

  • Feet rest fully on the floor or a footrest.
  • Thighs are roughly level and the seat edge does not press behind your knees.
  • Your lower back rests against the backrest.
  • Shoulders are relaxed and elbows stay by your sides.
  • Wrists are straight, not bent up or down.
  • You can see the screen without bending your head.

This is general guidance and does not replace advice from a doctor or specialist. If you have persistent pain, speak to a professional.
