Ergonomic keyboard buying guide: split, tenting and the learning curve

A standard keyboard pulls your hands together in front of you and turns your forearms fully palm-down. Ergonomic keyboards try to bring your hands closer to what OSHA calls a neutral position: hand, wrist and forearm in one line.

Split keyboards

The keys are divided into two halves, either in a single wave-shaped body or in two fully separate pieces. Separate halves can sit at shoulder width so your wrists do not bend outward. The more separation, the more flexibility, and the slightly longer the adjustment period.

Tenting

On a tented keyboard the middle is higher than the edges, like a low pitched roof. This reduces forearm rotation, because your hands turn slightly towards a handshake position.

Front-to-back slope

Cornell University’s guidance suggests a negative tilt, with the keyboard sloping slightly away from you. So do not flip out the rear feet. Some ergonomic keyboards include a palm rest designed with the same aim.

Take the learning curve seriously

Fully split or column-staggered keyboards slow your typing for days or weeks. If your work depends on fast typing, either adjust gradually or start with a one-piece wave keyboard, which changes less.

If you type in more than one language

Many ergonomic keyboards ship with English legends only. That is fine if you touch-type; otherwise consider keycap stickers or laser engraving. The layout itself is set in your operating system, not by the keyboard.

Before you buy

  • Size: do you need a number pad? A smaller keyboard brings the mouse closer.
  • Connection: wireless or wired, and how many devices it pairs with.
  • Programmable keys, if you use shortcuts heavily.
  • Return terms, because keyboard feel is entirely personal.

This is general guidance and does not replace advice from a doctor or specialist. If you have persistent pain, speak to a professional.
